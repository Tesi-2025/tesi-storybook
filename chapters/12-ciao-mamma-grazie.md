# Chapter 12 — Ciao Mamma, Grazie

The sky outside had gone the deep London blue that is not quite black, the kind of blue that only happens above a big damp city late at night. Somewhere far below on the street a fox screamed and a bus sighed and someone laughed. The kitchen was very quiet.

Babbo had cleared the espresso cups. All of them. He had also, without saying anything about it, washed each one by hand and set them upside down on a tea towel in a small neat parade. The laptop was closed. The printer, still broken, had been unplugged and given a small forgiving pat on the head. The presentation Donkey Shoes had been put back in their box.

They had eaten. Babbo had made pasta with the last of the good tomatoes and a great deal of parmesan, and everybody had eaten more than they meant to because forty-eight hours of adrenaline had left them, all at once and simultaneously, ravenously hungry. Topo had eaten his own weight in parmesan. Draghetta had toasted her marshmallows. Tesi had had one perfect grape.

Now it was bedtime.

Mamma sat on the big soft chair by the window — the coding chair, which was also, on nights like this, the family chair. She had her long legs tucked up. Babbo sat beside her, one arm around her shoulder. Draghetta was curled up in the crook of Mamma's other elbow, her small dragon wings folded, her breath steady, her nostrils safely closed. Topo was asleep on Mamma's collarbone, one paw over his eyes, snoring the specific little mouse-snore that sounds like a distant sewing machine.

Tesi was on the perch above them all, but he was not sleeping. He was watching.

He was watching the way the light from the streetlamp came through the window and made a soft gold rectangle on the carpet. He was watching Mamma's chest rise and fall. He was watching Babbo's hand, very gently, stroking Mamma's mane. He was watching his little brother snore and his little sister sleep, and he was — in the small ferocious way that thirty grams of parrotlet can love — loving them, all of them, so much that his chest felt three times its actual size.

"*Tesi,*" said Mamma quietly, without opening her eyes. "*Vieni qui, amore.*"

Tesi flew down. He landed on her chest, right over her heart, where he could feel the giraffe drum of her under his tiny feet. He tucked his head down. He puffed himself up. He became, briefly, the smallest possible fluffy green ball on the largest possible living heartbeat.

"*Grazie,*" whispered Mamma. "*Grazie per oggi. Grazie per ieri. Grazie per tutto.*"

"*Ciao, Mamma,*" chirped Tesi. Which meant many things. It meant *hello* and *goodnight* and *I love you* and *I will be here tomorrow and I will bite you again then, gently, because that is what I do.*

Mamma smiled with her eyes closed.

"*Ciao, piccolo. Ti voglio bene.*"

The advisory board slept. The startup slept. The application, somewhere in a data centre in California, waited patiently in a queue with a lot of other people's dreams. The rain against the window slowed and stopped. The London blue outside deepened into proper night.

And in a small warm flat above a small quiet street, a giraffe and her husband and their dragon-daughter and their mouse-son and their green parrotlet slept, piled together on one chair, breathing in the same slow rhythm, dreaming the same soft dream — of donkeys in comfortable shoes, walking calmly along good roads, going home.

*Fine.*
