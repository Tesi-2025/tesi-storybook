# Chapter 6 — Babbo's Espresso

It was, by then, three in the morning.

The kitchen looked like a small very focused refugee camp. Mamma's mane had gone into that particular tangle it goes into during long coding sessions, the one that made her look, according to Babbo, "like a beautiful electrocuted willow." There were now nine espresso cups on the table, plus two mugs, plus a small thimble that had once held Topo's caffeine and now held, somehow, a marshmallow. Draghetta was asleep with her chin on the keyboard, gently smouldering in her dreams. Topo was curled inside an empty biscuit tin, twitching through some operatic dream in which he was singing at La Scala.

Only Mamma and Tesi were still awake, and Mamma was beginning to type in the wrong language. She had just written the sentence "*perché footwear is critical for quadruped ergonomia*" and had not noticed.

Tesi noticed. Tesi always noticed. He walked across the keyboard and stood on the `esc` key with the firmness of a parrotlet who knows an intervention is required.

"*Non posso, amore,*" whispered Mamma. "*Un altro paragrafo. Devo finire.*"

Tesi did not move.

That was when Babbo appeared. He had, at some point, gone downstairs. He had, at some point, come back. He was now standing in the doorway holding a small silver tray. On the tray was one espresso — but not just any espresso. This espresso had been made with the deliberate attention of a man who understood exactly what a crisis needed. Perfect crema. Two grams of sugar. A very thin curl of lemon peel on the side of the saucer, because he had once read that the Neapolitans do this and he had never quite decided whether it was true but he liked the ritual of it.

"*Amore,*" he said. "*Bevi.*"

Mamma looked at the espresso the way a person looks at a lighthouse after a long night at sea.

She drank it in one small ceremonial sip.

Something happened. The kitchen went, very slightly, into higher resolution. The colours brightened. The laptop fan sounded, for a moment, more musical. Mamma's neck straightened. Her mane, without any visible intervention, un-tangled itself by approximately fifteen percent.

"*Mamma mia,*" she said, quietly.

"*Bravo Babbo,*" said Tesi, in his own language, which nobody in the kitchen technically understood but everybody in the kitchen always understood anyway.

Babbo sat down beside his wife. He put one large gentle paw on her shoulder. He did not say anything. He did not need to. He had co-founded a company with her because he loved her; he was building Donkey Shoes with her because he believed in her; and at three in the morning in a small kitchen in London he was bringing her espresso because he had promised, on their wedding day, to always be the person who brought her the thing she needed at the moment she needed it.

Mamma leaned her long neck sideways until her cheek rested, very briefly, against his.

"*Ti voglio bene,*" she said.

"*Ti voglio bene anch'io,*" he said.

Then she went back to work. But the paragraph she wrote next was in the correct language, and it was, everyone later agreed, the best paragraph in the entire application.
