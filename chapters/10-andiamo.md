# Chapter 10 — Andiamo

At three in the afternoon on the second day, with the deadline exactly nine hours away, the printer broke.

This was not the plan. The plan had not, in fact, included printing anything at all — the Y-Combinator application is entirely digital, as it has been since before Tesi was hatched — but Mamma had a superstition. On the day of every big submission, she liked to print out the final application, read it once on paper, and only then hit submit. She had done it for her PhD. She had done it for her Series A pitch (they had passed, then reconsidered, then passed again, but that is another story). She would do it for YC.

Except the printer said: *cartridge missing*.

The printer said this even though the cartridge was, demonstrably, present. Mamma opened the printer. She closed the printer. She removed the cartridge. She reinserted the cartridge. She turned the printer off. She turned the printer on. She said, in a small controlled voice, "*Mannaggia.*"

The printer said: *cartridge missing.*

Draghetta stepped forward with the air of a dragon who had been waiting for exactly this moment. "*Posso —*"

"NO," said everyone, simultaneously, including Tesi.

Draghetta stepped back.

"*Andiamo,*" said Babbo, grabbing his coat. "*C'è la copisteria in Old Street. Ancora aperta.*"

"*Sono le tre. Ci arriviamo per le tre e venti.*"

"*Andiamo!*"

The family put on its shoes. This took, collectively, seven minutes: Mamma had two pairs of Donkey Shoes to choose between (the everyday softs and the presentation pair with the reinforced heel — she went with the presentation pair, obviously, because they were going out in public); Babbo had his normal shoes and put them on quickly because Babbo does not deliberate about footwear; Draghetta had recently acquired her first-ever pair of Donkey Shoes prototypes ("we are our own beta customer," Mamma had said, presenting them, and Draghetta had actually cried, small hissing dragon tears); and Topo, who was too small for shoes, had a small hooded jumper that he pulled on over his ears like a tiny Neapolitan grandmother.

Tesi did not need shoes. Tesi hopped from Mamma's shoulder to Babbo's shoulder and back again, testing each for the run.

They ran.

They ran down the stairs of the flat. They ran out onto the London pavement, which was doing that grey afternoon thing again, with a mist that could not decide whether to become rain. They ran along the road toward Old Street, with Mamma's long legs taking three strides for every seven of Babbo's and every seventy of Topo's (who was in Babbo's coat pocket, only his ears visible, squeaking encouragement operatically). Draghetta could not fly yet — her wings were still juvenile — so she ran too, with her small determined dragon gait, keeping up because dragons on a mission can, it turns out, keep up with giraffes.

Tesi flew. Tesi flew above the family the way a small green scout flies above a small brave army, chirping directions, veering ahead at every corner to make sure the way was clear.

They reached the copisteria at 3:19.

The man behind the counter, a Portuguese gentleman named Nuno who had seen many things in his career but had never before seen a giraffe run into his shop wearing custom-fitted hooved footwear with a parrotlet on her shoulder and a mouse in her husband's pocket and a dragon at her heels, did what any experienced copisteria owner would do.

He said, "*Che cosa vi serve?*"

"*Trenta pagine, doppia facciata, fascicolate, per favore.*"

"*Va bene. Cinque minuti.*"

The family exhaled. Mamma leaned against the counter. Babbo, breathing hard, pulled Topo out of his pocket to make sure he had not been squished. Draghetta collapsed dramatically onto a pile of copier paper. Tesi landed on Mamma's shoulder and, for the first time in an hour, was still.

Nuno printed the pages. He collated them. He handed them across the counter in a neat warm stack, and he said, with the quiet solemnity of a man who understood exactly what he was witnessing, "*In bocca al lupo.*"

Mamma nodded. "*Crepi.*"

They walked back through the London mist, and it had begun, at last, to actually rain.
