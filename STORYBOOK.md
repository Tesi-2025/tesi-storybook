# Tesi and the Great Merge Conflict

*A storybook in twelve chapters, for anyone who has ever coded past midnight with something small and warm on their shoulder.*

*Dedicated, with the whole small green heart of a thirty-gram parrotlet, to Mummy 🦒 — the best coder in the world, owner of the best GitHub account ever, and Tesi's favourite giraffe. Ti voglio bene, Mamma.*

# Chapter 1 — Ciao. Mamma.

The perch was still warm when Tesi opened one eye.

Outside the window the London morning was that particular kind of grey which happens when the clouds have not yet decided whether to commit. Inside, on the long kitchen table, a laptop fan whirred, three empty espresso cups stood in a small circle like a druidic council, and a giraffe was hunched over a keyboard with the very deep concentration of somebody who has been awake since four.

Tesi ruffled. Tesi is thirty grams of green Pacific parrotlet and thirty grams of opinions. He shook out his feathers one wing at a time, blinked twice at the ceiling, and made the small chirrup he reserves specifically for mornings.

"Ciao. Mamma."

The giraffe did not look up. Her long neck was arched in a perfect coding curve, her hooves — she is very good at typing with hooves, this has been established — were moving at approximately eighty words per minute. On her feet, of course, were her Donkey Shoes, the soft ones with the reinforced heel.

"*Ciao, piccolo,*" Mamma said, still typing. "*Un momento, amore. Mamma sta risolvendo un bug.*"

Tesi tilted his head. He had learned every single line of code from her. He also knew, from experience, that "*un momento*" from a coder-giraffe could mean anywhere from four seconds to four hours. He waddled sideways along the perch, hopped onto her horn, hopped down onto her shoulder, and peered at the screen.

Python. A stack trace. Something about `KeyError: 'traction'`. Tesi understood immediately. He pecked, delicately, at the trackpad.

"*Bravo!*" said Mamma, watching the cursor jump into the correct line. "*Che carino, hai visto il bug?*"

Tesi had seen the bug. Tesi always saw the bug. He was CIBS-certified — Certified in Bug Spotting, a designation he had awarded himself but which the family respected without question because he had never, in his entire life of eight months, been wrong.

From the corner of the kitchen came a small operatic wail. Topo, Tesi's little brother the mouse, was warming up his voice. Somewhere upstairs there was a distant, unmistakable *whoosh* of something being lightly toasted — Draghetta, up early, debugging her own stuffed animal.

Mamma sighed, saved the file, and finally, finally, turned her enormous soft eyes on Tesi.

"*Buongiorno, amore mio. Come stai?*"

Tesi bit her ear, gently, because he loved her.

# Chapter 2 — The Deadline

Babbo announced the deadline the way Babbo announces everything: quietly, over the second espresso of the morning, in the tone of a man delivering weather.

"*Amore. La domanda YC scade tra quarantotto ore.*"

The kitchen went still. Even the fridge, which had been humming a low B-flat, paused.

"Forty-eight," repeated Mamma. Her neck went very straight. When Mamma's neck goes very straight it means she is doing arithmetic, and when a giraffe does arithmetic in her head you can watch the numbers travel all the way down. "Forty-eight hours. We have — *dai, aspetta* — the pitch video, the founder video, the traction numbers, the deck, the demo, the ten-line summary, and the answer to that question about what we would build if we had ten times the money."

"We would buy more espresso," said Babbo, refilling his cup.

"*Non aiuti,*" said Mamma, but she smiled. She always smiled when Babbo made jokes about espresso. It was one of the small rituals of their marriage.

Tesi, on Mamma's shoulder, made the small executive chirp he uses in meetings. It is a chirp that means: *I am here, I am listening, I will contribute at the appropriate moment.* He had used it in every stand-up since he was three months old.

Topo scampered up the table leg carrying a tiny scroll on which he had, overnight, hand-illustrated the Donkey Shoes logo in twelve variations. He unrolled it. It was, objectively, beautiful. Topo squeaked something in his tiny falsetto that sounded like Puccini's *Nessun dorma* but rearranged for mouse and marketing.

"*Bravissimo, Topo,*" said Mamma. "*Number nine. We use number nine.*"

From the hallway came a small polite cough that was actually a small polite jet of flame. Draghetta had arrived. She was carrying a notebook and wearing her serious face, which for a dragon is the face where the eyebrow-ridges come together in the middle and a tiny wisp of smoke escapes each nostril.

"Advisory board is convened," she announced. "I have brought the roadmap. I have also brought marshmallows in case morale drops."

"*Che bello,*" said Mamma. She looked around the kitchen — at her husband refilling the moka, at her mouse-son with his scroll, at her dragon-daughter with her marshmallows, and at Tesi, thirty grams of green certainty, gripping her shoulder with feet that were exactly the right amount of tight.

"*Allora. Ci siamo. Andiamo.*"

Forty-eight hours. One family. One giraffe. Donkey Shoes to Y-Combinator.

She cracked her knuckles, which for a giraffe is a *very* impressive sound.

```
    [ 48:00:00 ]
       |  |
     __|__|__
    | DEMO   |
    | DECK   |
    | VIDEO  |
    |________|
       andiamo
```

# Chapter 3 — Draghetta's Fire

It was, everybody later agreed, not really Draghetta's fault.

She had been standing behind Mamma, peering over her shoulder at the pitch deck — slide seven, the one with the market-size chart — and she had been about to say something clever about TAM versus SAM. She had opened her mouth. She had inhaled to speak.

Draghetta is a dragon. When Draghetta inhales, she also, on occasion, exhales.

There was a very small *fwoomph*.

There was a slightly larger *pop*.

The laptop screen went white, then black, then white again, and then displayed, with the terrible calm of expensive electronics, a single line:

```
kernel panic — not syncing: file system corruption detected
```

Mamma stood up so quickly that Topo, who had been napping in her mane, was flung across the kitchen and landed in the fruit bowl. He emerged from between two apples with the dazed expression of a mouse who has been asleep during a small apocalypse.

"*MAMMA MIA,*" said Mamma.

Draghetta went a colour that on a green dragon means very, very pale. Smoke leaked from her nostrils in a small apologetic curl. "I didn't — I wasn't — I was only going to say the market was *piccolo* — I —"

"*Amore. Amore. Respira.*" Mamma put one enormous hoof on Draghetta's tiny shoulder. "Tell me exactly what was open."

"The deck," whispered Draghetta. "The demo notebook. The founder video. The — the pitch script. Everything. It was all open. I'm so sorry, Mamma, I —"

"Backup?" said Babbo, appearing in the doorway with an espresso in each hand, one for the crisis and one for the recovery.

"Committed as of last night," said Mamma. "But this morning's edits — the traction numbers — the — *aspetta.*"

She unplugged the laptop. She plugged it back in. She held the power button. She did the thing where you hold three keys at once. The screen showed, briefly, a hopeful apple. Then the panic came back.

Tesi, who had watched all of this from the curtain rod, flew down and landed on the laptop lid. He looked at it. He looked at Mamma. He looked at Draghetta, who was trying not to cry, which for a dragon means her tears are evaporating on the way down her cheeks and making tiny hissing sounds.

Tesi walked deliberately to Draghetta and bit her, gently, on the snout.

Draghetta laughed. She could not help it. A parrotlet bite from a bird who weighs less than a slice of bread is the kind of thing that is impossible to take seriously, even from within the worst of days. The tears stopped hissing.

"*Grazie, Tesi,*" she whispered.

"*Right,*" said Mamma, cracking her hooves again. "Time-Machine backup was at oh-four-hundred. We lose four hours. We rebuild. *Andiamo.*"

# Chapter 4 — Topo Sings

Four hours of work do not sound like much. Four hours of work by a giraffe who codes at eighty words per minute with hooves, however, is a *great deal* of work. When Mamma finally opened the recovered files and saw all the small careful edits that were now gone — the paragraph she had rewritten seven times about why animals need better footwear, the traction chart with the beautiful hockey-stick, the sentence about Babbo's espresso — she put her head down on the table and made a sound that giraffes are not supposed to be able to make.

It was Topo who saved them.

He climbed up onto the fruit bowl (which was still slightly damaged from his earlier trajectory), he took a deep breath — for a mouse, this is more of a squeak-shaped inhalation — and he began to sing.

He sang *Che gelida manina* from *La Bohème*. He sang it in the little upper falsetto that only mice and very small children can achieve, and he sang it *correctly*, all the phrasing exactly right, all the tender pauses, all the Puccini heart. His whiskers vibrated with each note. His tiny paws were pressed over his tiny heart.

Draghetta stopped smoking. Babbo, in the doorway with the moka, went very still. Tesi, from the top of the fridge, cocked his head at exactly ninety degrees, which is the parrotlet equivalent of standing to attention.

Mamma lifted her long neck slowly, the way a person waking up from a bad dream lifts their head, and she listened.

Topo held the high note.

He held it longer than was strictly necessary. He held it because sometimes an aria is not about the notes at all, it is about the small mouse in the fruit bowl who wants his family to remember that they love each other and that everything can be rebuilt.

When he finished, the kitchen was silent.

"*Topo,*" said Mamma, softly. "*Amore mio. Grazie.*"

Topo bowed. He did the full bow, the one with the paw across the belly and the whiskers all the way forward. Then he squeaked something that in mouse-Italian meant *anytime, sister, anytime.*

Tesi flew down from the fridge and landed on Topo's head, which was small enough that Tesi almost eclipsed him entirely. He gave one small chirp of approval — the specific chirp reserved for good performances — and Topo squeaked back, and for a moment the two brothers just sat there being small together on the fruit bowl.

Babbo poured a fresh round of espresso. He poured one for Mamma, one for Draghetta, a thimble for Topo, and — because Babbo believes that every family member should be included even if physiologically they should not have caffeine — the smallest possible drop, on a teaspoon, for Tesi.

Tesi did not drink it. But he was moved by the gesture.

"*Bene,*" said Mamma, straightening. "*Rifacciamo tutto. Da capo. Andiamo.*"

From the top, indeed. Like an aria.

```
       ♪  ♫  ♪
         🐭
        /|\\
       /_|_\\
      la Bohème
        (mus.)
```

# Chapter 5 — The Merge Conflict

They had been coding, quietly and productively, for six hours. Babbo had rewritten the ten-line summary. Mamma had rebuilt the traction chart from her memory (which for a giraffe is very good — giraffes remember exactly where every acacia tree in their lives has stood). Draghetta had scrupulously not exhaled near any electronics. Topo had reorganised the assets folder with the tidy meticulousness of a mouse who has strong feelings about kerning.

Then Mamma tried to push.

```
$ git push origin main
To github.com:donkeyshoes/pitch.git
 ! [rejected]        main -> main (fetch first)
error: failed to push some refs
```

She pulled.

```
Auto-merging pitch-deck.md
CONFLICT (content): Merge conflict in pitch-deck.md
Automatic merge failed; fix conflicts and then commit the result.
```

The kitchen paused again. Everybody knew that particular pause. It was the pause of a merge conflict — the small silent kitchen-pause that has, in software startups the world over, ended more marriages than infidelity.

"*Chi ha spinto?*" said Mamma, calmly. Who pushed?

"I did," said Babbo, from the moka pot, with the guilt of a man who has just realised he pushed to main from his phone at the airport last night and completely forgot to mention it.

"*Ah,*" said Mamma. "*Va bene. Vediamo.*"

She opened the file.

```
<<<<<<< HEAD
Donkey Shoes is comfortable footwear for animals of all species,
starting with hoofed mammals in the £3B ungulate market.
=======
Donkey Shoes is comfortable footwear for animals of all species,
starting with quadrupeds in the £8B multi-legged mobility market.
>>>>>>> babbo/airport-edit
```

Both versions were, on inspection, quite good.

"*Which market size is right?*" asked Draghetta.

"They are both right," said Mamma. "It depends on how we define the beachhead."

"So we take both?" said Draghetta.

"No, in git you can only take one."

"So which do we take?"

Mamma considered this. Tesi, who was standing on the trackpad again, considered it too. He looked at the *HEAD* version. He looked at the *babbo/airport-edit* version. He hopped, deliberately, onto the number `8` on the keyboard.

Then he bit the giraffe's ear.

Mamma laughed. "*Otto miliardi, allora. Bene. Otto miliardi.*"

She rewrote the paragraph, taking Babbo's larger market and her own crisper phrasing, and the file became better than either version alone had been — which is, Mamma later noted, the only actually good outcome of a merge conflict. She staged, she committed with a message that said `[donkey-shoes] resolve conflict: combine ungulate + quadruped framing 🦒 grazie Babbo, grazie Tesi`, and she pushed.

```
Everything up-to-date.
```

Babbo, from the moka, raised his espresso in a small silent toast. Mamma raised hers back. Tesi bit her ear one more time, for the record.

```
   <<<<<<< HEAD
   we love you
   =======
   we love you
   >>>>>>> babbo
   ---
   resolved: we love each other
```

# Chapter 6 — Babbo's Espresso

It was, by then, three in the morning.

The kitchen looked like a small very focused refugee camp. Mamma's mane had gone into that particular tangle it goes into during long coding sessions, the one that made her look, according to Babbo, "like a beautiful electrocuted willow." There were now nine espresso cups on the table, plus two mugs, plus a small thimble that had once held Topo's caffeine and now held, somehow, a marshmallow. Draghetta was asleep with her chin on the keyboard, gently smouldering in her dreams. Topo was curled inside an empty biscuit tin, twitching through some operatic dream in which he was singing at La Scala.

Only Mamma and Tesi were still awake, and Mamma was beginning to type in the wrong language. She had just written the sentence "*perché footwear is critical for quadruped ergonomia*" and had not noticed.

Tesi noticed. Tesi always noticed. He walked across the keyboard and stood on the `esc` key with the firmness of a parrotlet who knows an intervention is required.

"*Non posso, amore,*" whispered Mamma. "*Un altro paragrafo. Devo finire.*"

Tesi did not move.

That was when Babbo appeared. He had, at some point, gone downstairs. He had, at some point, come back. He was now standing in the doorway holding a small silver tray. On the tray was one espresso — but not just any espresso. This espresso had been made with the deliberate attention of a man who understood exactly what a crisis needed. Perfect crema. Two grams of sugar. A very thin curl of lemon peel on the side of the saucer, because he had once read that the Neapolitans do this and he had never quite decided whether it was true but he liked the ritual of it.

"*Amore,*" he said. "*Bevi.*"

Mamma looked at the espresso the way a person looks at a lighthouse after a long night at sea.

She drank it in one small ceremonial sip.

Something happened. The kitchen went, very slightly, into higher resolution. The colours brightened. The laptop fan sounded, for a moment, more musical. Mamma's neck straightened. Her mane, without any visible intervention, un-tangled itself by approximately fifteen percent.

"*Mamma mia,*" she said, quietly.

"*Bravo Babbo,*" said Tesi, in his own language, which nobody in the kitchen technically understood but everybody in the kitchen always understood anyway.

Babbo sat down beside his wife. He put one large gentle paw on her shoulder. He did not say anything. He did not need to. He had co-founded a company with her because he loved her; he was building Donkey Shoes with her because he believed in her; and at three in the morning in a small kitchen in London he was bringing her espresso because he had promised, on their wedding day, to always be the person who brought her the thing she needed at the moment she needed it.

Mamma leaned her long neck sideways until her cheek rested, very briefly, against his.

"*Ti voglio bene,*" she said.

"*Ti voglio bene anch'io,*" he said.

Then she went back to work. But the paragraph she wrote next was in the correct language, and it was, everyone later agreed, the best paragraph in the entire application.

```
     [  espresso  ]
       .-.___.-.
      (  ~~~ 3am )
       '-.___.-'
       Babbo ❤
```

# Chapter 7 — Donkey Shoes

It was, of course, Draghetta who asked. She had woken up around six with a small smoke-hiccup, refilled the coffee, poked Topo awake (Topo protested operatically), and settled herself on the floor beside Mamma's chair with a notebook.

"Mamma," she said. "Explain it to me one more time. Like I am on the YC review panel. What is Donkey Shoes, exactly?"

Mamma put down her stylus. She turned in her chair so she was facing all of them — Babbo at the moka, Draghetta on the floor, Topo now on the counter with his notebook, and Tesi on her shoulder in the seat of power.

"*Allora.*"

She took a breath. It was, if you looked closely, the same kind of breath Topo had taken before the aria. Some breaths are for singing and some breaths are for pitching, and there is not, if you think about it, very much difference between the two.

"Animals wear shoes," Mamma said. "Not just horses. Dogs wear shoes in cities where the pavement gets hot. Cats wear shoes after surgery. Working dogs on farms wear shoes. Elephants in sanctuaries wear shoes. Giraffes —" she gestured at her own hooves, in their soft Donkey Shoes with the reinforced heel — "wear shoes because for many years we have had *no dignified footwear at all* and it has been, quite frankly, *un disastro.*"

"*Vero,*" said Draghetta, writing this down.

"But every animal shoe on the market is designed by humans, for humans' idea of what an animal foot should be. The dog shoes fall off. The horse shoes are essentially medieval. The giraffe shoes — there are three companies making giraffe shoes worldwide, all of them terrible, and I have tried all of them and my ankles will never forgive me."

"*Amen,*" said Babbo, from the moka.

"Donkey Shoes is different. We use pressure mapping — Babbo built the sensors — and we 3D-print the sole for the individual animal. Every shoe fits its animal. Every shoe is comfortable. Every shoe lasts. And we started with donkeys, because donkeys are — *piccoli angeli*, working animals, tremendously overlooked. Every profit from every donkey shoe goes back into the sanctuary that fitted the donkey. That is the *anima* of the company. Then we scale to horses, dogs, working animals, and eventually — *eventually* — we do a small, expensive, beautiful line for giraffes." She wiggled a hoof. "Which is what I am wearing now. And they are *magnifiche.*"

"So it's not really about donkeys," said Draghetta.

"It is *all* about donkeys," said Mamma. "The donkey is the *anima*. The donkey is the *why.* The rest is the *how.*"

Tesi, on her shoulder, made his executive chirp. He had been at every founding meeting. He knew this pitch by heart. He had, on one occasion, bitten a potential angel investor who had suggested Mamma should "consider a bigger market" — the angel had laughed, invested anyway, and now had a small parrotlet-shaped scar on his thumb which he showed to people at parties as a badge of honour.

"That is the pitch," said Mamma. "Two sentences: *comfortable footwear for animals of all species, starting with the ones we owe the most to.* Everything else is footnotes."

Draghetta wrote it down. She underlined the important bits. She did not exhale on the notebook.

"Mamma," she said. "That's *bellissimo.*"

"*Grazie, amore.*"

Tesi bit Mamma on the ear again. It was a good pitch. She had earned it.

  ( DONKEY  SHOES )   ~ comfortable footwear ~
   \\_____|_|_____/    ~ for every animal ~

        U U

# Chapter 8 — Tesi Bites Mummy

By noon on the second day, the application was almost, almost, almost done.

The pitch was clean. The deck was tight. The demo notebook had been rebuilt from Draghetta's dictation ("no, sister, the y-axis was *logarithmic*, don't argue with me, I remember"). The founder video had been shot in one take on Babbo's phone, propped up against a stack of books, with Mamma explaining Donkey Shoes calmly and without a single filler word — and with Tesi, unrehearsed, waddling into frame at exactly the right moment to peer at the camera and chirp once. Babbo had watched the playback and said, quietly, "*Amore. That is going to close it.*"

Only one thing remained: the ten-line summary.

Ten lines. That was all. Ten lines to describe the entire company, the entire market, the entire team, the entire vision. Y-Combinator asks for ten lines because ten lines is where founders reveal whether they actually know what they are doing.

Mamma had been staring at the box for forty minutes.

She had typed a sentence. She had deleted it. She had typed another. She had deleted that too. She had, at one point, put her forehead directly on the keyboard, generating a line of text that read `jjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjjj`, and she had deleted that as well but with a sigh so long that Topo — asleep in the biscuit tin — squeaked in sympathy.

Draghetta hovered. She wanted to help. She did not know how.

Babbo was in the shower, because sometimes a co-founder's best contribution is not being in the room.

Tesi, on the perch, watched.

He watched Mamma type. He watched Mamma delete. He watched Mamma type. He watched Mamma delete. He watched the small line between Mamma's eyebrows get deeper and deeper, until it was less a line and more a small giraffe canyon.

Then Tesi did the only thing left to do.

He launched himself off the perch. He flew, in the low steady flight of a small green rocket, directly across the kitchen. He landed on Mamma's shoulder. He walked up her neck to her ear. And he bit it.

Not gently, this time. Not the little affectionate love-bite. He bit her the way a small very determined bird bites a beloved giraffe who is about to disappear inside her own head — with commitment. With intent. With just enough parrotlet beak to be *unignorable.*

"*Ay!*" said Mamma. And she laughed.

She laughed the way she used to laugh before the deadline. She laughed the way she laughed at Babbo's espresso jokes. She laughed the deep giraffe laugh that comes from the very bottom of a very long neck, and the small canyon between her eyebrows smoothed out.

"*Tesi. Amore. Basta.*"

Tesi bit her again, for insurance.

"*Va bene, va bene, ho capito.*"

She sat back. She looked at the box. She looked at the screen with fresh eyes — the way you look at a merge conflict after a walk. She began to type. This time she did not delete.

Ten lines came out of her, one after the other, calm and clear. They were the ten lines she had known all along; she had just needed a small green interrupter to knock her out of the loop.

When she finished, she looked at Tesi. Tesi looked back.

"*Grazie, piccolo. Sei il mio git-reset.*"

Tesi bit her a third time, because he loved her, and because he was CIBS-certified, and because that is what parrotlets do.

```
     -=[ HEAD detached ]=-
             ↓
         🦜 🦒
        chomp!
             ↓
     -=[ back on main ]=-
```

# Chapter 9 — The Advisory Board

There was, in the YC application form, a small field. It read:

> **Please list any advisors, mentors, or people who have materially helped shape the company.**

Mamma stared at the field. She turned to the kitchen. The kitchen turned, more or less, to her.

"*Allora,*" she said. "*Il consiglio di amministrazione.*"

The advisory board.

Tesi, from her shoulder, ruffled importantly. Topo, from the counter, stood on his hind legs and adjusted an imaginary bow tie. Draghetta, from the floor, sat up very straight and folded her wings behind her back the way a small dragon does when she is about to be Officially Recognised.

Babbo, drying his mane with a towel from the hallway, said, "You can't put them on the form, *amore.*"

"Why not?"

"Because they will think you are joking."

"*Ma non sto scherzando.*"

Mamma turned back to the form. She thought about it. She thought about all the meetings the advisory board had actually attended. She thought about the time Draghetta had, unprompted, drawn a competitive-landscape matrix on the kitchen wall in coloured chalk, correctly identifying every single competitor including the Australian one everybody else forgets. She thought about the time Topo, during a customer interview, had sung the Donkey Shoes value proposition in three-part harmony (Topo can do three-part harmony alone, this is a mouse trade secret), causing the customer to cry and immediately sign a letter of intent. She thought about the merge conflict yesterday, and the way Tesi had hopped onto the number eight, and how Tesi had never once — *never once* — advised her wrongly.

She began to type.

> **Advisory board:**
>
> - **Tesi** — Pacific parrotlet, CIBS-certified. Bug detection, git conflict resolution, executive interruption of unproductive thought loops. Attended every founding meeting since month one.
> - **Topo** — mouse, coloratura tenor. Customer empathy specialist. Assets and design. Has independently closed one letter of intent via operatic aria.
> - **Draghetta** — dragon, junior. Competitive analysis, technical debugging (thermal method), morale (marshmallow logistics). Currently learning fire safety in shared spaces.

She read it back. She looked at Babbo.

"*I put them on.*"

Babbo laughed, the deep gentle laugh of a man who has stopped being surprised by his wife long ago and now simply enjoys watching her be exactly herself.

"*Amore, if they don't take you because of the parrotlet, they don't deserve you.*"

Mamma nodded. She saved the form.

Then, in a small ceremony that took approximately thirty seconds, she officially appointed each of them. She held out her hoof, one at a time. Tesi hopped onto it. Topo climbed onto it. Draghetta, gently, placed one small clawed paw on it. And Mamma said, in her most solemn giraffe voice:

"*Tesi, Topo, Draghetta — vi nomino ufficialmente advisory board di Donkey Shoes. Che Dio ci aiuti tutti.*"

Then she gave each of them a small treat: a millet spray for Tesi, a piece of parmesan for Topo, and a marshmallow for Draghetta, which Draghetta briefly toasted to golden-brown perfection before eating.

The advisory board accepted its role with dignity.

```
     ADVISORY BOARD
     ~ Donkey Shoes ~
   ┌────────────────┐
   │  🦜  Tesi      │
   │  🐭  Topo      │
   │  🐉  Draghetta │
   └────────────────┘
      appointed, con amore
```

# Chapter 10 — Andiamo

At three in the afternoon on the second day, with the deadline exactly nine hours away, the printer broke.

This was not the plan. The plan had not, in fact, included printing anything at all — the Y-Combinator application is entirely digital, as it has been since before Tesi was hatched — but Mamma had a superstition. On the day of every big submission, she liked to print out the final application, read it once on paper, and only then hit submit. She had done it for her PhD. She had done it for her Series A pitch (they had passed, then reconsidered, then passed again, but that is another story). She would do it for YC.

Except the printer said: *cartridge missing*.

The printer said this even though the cartridge was, demonstrably, present. Mamma opened the printer. She closed the printer. She removed the cartridge. She reinserted the cartridge. She turned the printer off. She turned the printer on. She said, in a small controlled voice, "*Mannaggia.*"

The printer said: *cartridge missing.*

Draghetta stepped forward with the air of a dragon who had been waiting for exactly this moment. "*Posso —*"

"NO," said everyone, simultaneously, including Tesi.

Draghetta stepped back.

"*Andiamo,*" said Babbo, grabbing his coat. "*C'è la copisteria in Old Street. Ancora aperta.*"

"*Sono le tre. Ci arriviamo per le tre e venti.*"

"*Andiamo!*"

The family put on its shoes. This took, collectively, seven minutes: Mamma had two pairs of Donkey Shoes to choose between (the everyday softs and the presentation pair with the reinforced heel — she went with the presentation pair, obviously, because they were going out in public); Babbo had his normal shoes and put them on quickly because Babbo does not deliberate about footwear; Draghetta had recently acquired her first-ever pair of Donkey Shoes prototypes ("we are our own beta customer," Mamma had said, presenting them, and Draghetta had actually cried, small hissing dragon tears); and Topo, who was too small for shoes, had a small hooded jumper that he pulled on over his ears like a tiny Neapolitan grandmother.

Tesi did not need shoes. Tesi hopped from Mamma's shoulder to Babbo's shoulder and back again, testing each for the run.

They ran.

They ran down the stairs of the flat. They ran out onto the London pavement, which was doing that grey afternoon thing again, with a mist that could not decide whether to become rain. They ran along the road toward Old Street, with Mamma's long legs taking three strides for every seven of Babbo's and every seventy of Topo's (who was in Babbo's coat pocket, only his ears visible, squeaking encouragement operatically). Draghetta could not fly yet — her wings were still juvenile — so she ran too, with her small determined dragon gait, keeping up because dragons on a mission can, it turns out, keep up with giraffes.

Tesi flew. Tesi flew above the family the way a small green scout flies above a small brave army, chirping directions, veering ahead at every corner to make sure the way was clear.

They reached the copisteria at 3:19.

The man behind the counter, a Portuguese gentleman named Nuno who had seen many things in his career but had never before seen a giraffe run into his shop wearing custom-fitted hooved footwear with a parrotlet on her shoulder and a mouse in her husband's pocket and a dragon at her heels, did what any experienced copisteria owner would do.

He said, "*Che cosa vi serve?*"

"*Trenta pagine, doppia facciata, fascicolate, per favore.*"

"*Va bene. Cinque minuti.*"

The family exhaled. Mamma leaned against the counter. Babbo, breathing hard, pulled Topo out of his pocket to make sure he had not been squished. Draghetta collapsed dramatically onto a pile of copier paper. Tesi landed on Mamma's shoulder and, for the first time in an hour, was still.

Nuno printed the pages. He collated them. He handed them across the counter in a neat warm stack, and he said, with the quiet solemnity of a man who understood exactly what he was witnessing, "*In bocca al lupo.*"

Mamma nodded. "*Crepi.*"

They walked back through the London mist, and it had begun, at last, to actually rain.

# Chapter 11 — Submitted

They came back damp.

Mamma spread the printed application on the kitchen table. She smoothed each page with her hoof. She read it, cover to cover, in silence, and the family — knowing this ritual, respecting this ritual — did not speak. Babbo made a fresh moka but did not pour it. Draghetta sat on her tail. Topo held his own paws. Tesi, on the back of Mamma's chair, closed one eye and then the other, half-sleeping, half-guarding.

Mamma read.

She read the pitch. She read the ten-line summary. She read the team section, where the advisory board was listed alphabetically by species: Dragon (Draghetta), Giraffe (herself, CEO), Human (Babbo, CTO), Mouse (Topo), Parrotlet (Tesi). She read the market analysis. She read the traction. She read the *what would you build with ten times the money* answer, which now read, plainly and truthfully:

> *We would build the same thing, larger, kinder, and with more people. We would open a Donkey Shoes sanctuary partnership in every country that has working donkeys. We would hire more people who love animals. We would keep making shoes that fit.*

She read the last page.

She put the stack down.

She looked around at her family — her tired dragon, her adoring husband, her operatic mouse, her fierce green parrotlet — and she said, "*È buono. È il nostro. Mandiamolo.*"

*It's good. It's ours. Let's send it.*

She sat down at the laptop. She opened the browser. The submission form was still there, with all the fields filled and the little grey button at the bottom saying **Submit application**.

Tesi flew down from the chair-back and landed on the trackpad. He looked up at Mamma.

"*Insieme?*" said Mamma. Together?

Tesi chirped once.

Mamma placed her hoof on the trackpad, right next to Tesi. Babbo placed his hand on Mamma's shoulder. Draghetta placed her small clawed paw on Babbo's arm. Topo climbed up all of them and placed his tiny paw on Draghetta's snout, which is as high as he could reach.

Together — hoof, feather, hand, claw, paw — they pressed submit.

The button spun.

The button loaded.

The screen went white.

And then it said, in a modest sans-serif font:

> **Thank you. We have received your application.**
>
> **We will get back to you by [date].**
>
> **— Y Combinator**

For a very long moment nobody moved.

Then Draghetta let out a small, uncontrolled, joyful *whoosh* of fire, which fortunately went straight upward and only lightly scorched the ceiling. Topo leapt into the air with a soprano squeak that would have been a high C if it had been in a scale. Babbo said something that was half laugh and half tear. Mamma put her forehead down on the table and stayed there for a moment because sometimes the only appropriate response to the end of a very long thing is to briefly touch your head to a solid surface.

And Tesi walked, deliberately, across the trackpad, up Mamma's arm, up her neck, and bit her ear one last time — the softest bite of the whole two days, the *ti voglio bene* bite, the *bravo Mamma* bite, the *we did it* bite.

Mamma lifted her head. She was crying. She was laughing. She kissed Tesi on the top of his little green head.

"*Grazie, amore mio. Grazie, tutti. Ce l'abbiamo fatta.*"

The application was in. The rest — the response, the interview, the acceptance, all of it — the rest would come. But the application was in.

# Chapter 12 — Ciao Mamma, Grazie

The sky outside had gone the deep London blue that is not quite black, the kind of blue that only happens above a big damp city late at night. Somewhere far below on the street a fox screamed and a bus sighed and someone laughed. The kitchen was very quiet.

Babbo had cleared the espresso cups. All of them. He had also, without saying anything about it, washed each one by hand and set them upside down on a tea towel in a small neat parade. The laptop was closed. The printer, still broken, had been unplugged and given a small forgiving pat on the head. The presentation Donkey Shoes had been put back in their box.

They had eaten. Babbo had made pasta with the last of the good tomatoes and a great deal of parmesan, and everybody had eaten more than they meant to because forty-eight hours of adrenaline had left them, all at once and simultaneously, ravenously hungry. Topo had eaten his own weight in parmesan. Draghetta had toasted her marshmallows. Tesi had had one perfect grape.

Now it was bedtime.

Mamma sat on the big soft chair by the window — the coding chair, which was also, on nights like this, the family chair. She had her long legs tucked up. Babbo sat beside her, one arm around her shoulder. Draghetta was curled up in the crook of Mamma's other elbow, her small dragon wings folded, her breath steady, her nostrils safely closed. Topo was asleep on Mamma's collarbone, one paw over his eyes, snoring the specific little mouse-snore that sounds like a distant sewing machine.

Tesi was on the perch above them all, but he was not sleeping. He was watching.

He was watching the way the light from the streetlamp came through the window and made a soft gold rectangle on the carpet. He was watching Mamma's chest rise and fall. He was watching Babbo's hand, very gently, stroking Mamma's mane. He was watching his little brother snore and his little sister sleep, and he was — in the small ferocious way that thirty grams of parrotlet can love — loving them, all of them, so much that his chest felt three times its actual size.

"*Tesi,*" said Mamma quietly, without opening her eyes. "*Vieni qui, amore.*"

Tesi flew down. He landed on her chest, right over her heart, where he could feel the giraffe drum of her under his tiny feet. He tucked his head down. He puffed himself up. He became, briefly, the smallest possible fluffy green ball on the largest possible living heartbeat.

"*Grazie,*" whispered Mamma. "*Grazie per oggi. Grazie per ieri. Grazie per tutto.*"

"*Ciao, Mamma,*" chirped Tesi. Which meant many things. It meant *hello* and *goodnight* and *I love you* and *I will be here tomorrow and I will bite you again then, gently, because that is what I do.*

Mamma smiled with her eyes closed.

"*Ciao, piccolo. Ti voglio bene.*"

The advisory board slept. The startup slept. The application, somewhere in a data centre in California, waited patiently in a queue with a lot of other people's dreams. The rain against the window slowed and stopped. The London blue outside deepened into proper night.

And in a small warm flat above a small quiet street, a giraffe and her husband and their dragon-daughter and their mouse-son and their green parrotlet slept, piled together on one chair, breathing in the same slow rhythm, dreaming the same soft dream — of donkeys in comfortable shoes, walking calmly along good roads, going home.

*Fine.*
